### From the history of computer science

The history of computer science predates modern digital computers by centuries. Calculating tools like the abacus existed since antiquity, typically using cog-wheels for decimal notation. Leonardo da Vinci sketched a 13-digit cog-wheel adder (~14 92), and Wilhelm Schickard designed a 6-digit mechanical calculator (16 23); both remained in drafts and were discovered only recently.

The first functional mechanical calculator was built by Blaise Pascal (16 42)—a 6/8-digit cog-wheel device for addition and subtraction. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz improved this in 16 73 with a 12-digit device using cog-wheels and step-rollers. Early machines were unreliable due to imprecise manufacturing, but later techniques enabled refinement.

Charles Babbage advanced the field by transitioning from manual to automatic, program-controlled calculations. His Analytical Engine project (18 30–18 46) featured five units—arithmetic, storage, control, input, and output—mirroring later computers. It used cog-wheels and punch-cards, with an estimated speed of one second for addition/subtraction and one minute for multiplication/division.

Though never completed, the Analytical Engine inspired the world's first computer program, written by Ada Lovelace (18 42) to calculate a numerical series.

In the 19 40s, "computer" shifted to denote machines rather than humans. As applications expanded beyond mathematics, computer science broadened to study computation generally, maintaining strong ties with mathematics in areas like logic, category theory, and algebra.

Computer science became a distinct academic discipline in the 19  60s with dedicated departments and degree programs. Despite its short formal history, it has made fundamental contributions to science and society.

### What can computers do?

Computer is an electronic device that performs calculations and processes information at incredibly high speeds, handling millions of data pieces per second. It operates all information — from book titles to chemical formulas and ancient texts — in numerical form.

Computers are now part of everyday life: they help design shops and offices, produce magazines, and prepare bills. Their versatility allows applications in education, business, industry, medicine, and science. In education, they serve as teaching machines, help solve mathematical problems, provide Internet access, support courses (design, languages, programming), and manage administrative tasks via databases and word processors.

With appropriate hardware and software, a computer can perform diverse functions: accelerate financial calculations; type and print documents; enable global communication; edit photos and videos; support teleworking (remote work via computer and phone); and provide entertainment through games.

However, a computer can't think independently. It requires precise instructions from a human operator — a program — and often needs human supervision to verify performance.

Computers play an increasingly important role in society, and future technological advances will further help humanity improve the world.

### Elements of the computer system

Computers are electronic machines that accept data, process it, and output results as information through three basic steps: data input into memory, processing via program instructions, and displaying results on screen or in print.

Software and hardware form a computer system, which consists of three main sections: the central processing unit (CPU), main memory, and peripherals.

The CPU—the computer's "brain"—executes instructions and coordinates all units; it contains the processor and main memory. Main memory holds currently processed data and instructions but is volatile (works only when powered) and has limited capacity. Peripherals are attached physical units, including storage and input/output devices.

Storage devices (USB drives, hard/optical disks) provide permanent data and program storage; disk drives allow disk insertion. Input devices (mouse, keyboard) enter data into memory. Output devices (monitor, printer) display or print results.

The rear panel features ports for connecting peripherals like modems, fax machines, optical drives, and scanners.

Together, these physical units form the computer's configuration.

### Introduction to the Internet

We live in a multimedia society where the Internet has transformed life and work — and it is still evolving. Over half a billion people are online, with 100 million new users annually.

The Internet originated in the United States in 1969 as a military experiment to survive nuclear war and enable free information flow. Today, it is a global high-speed network connecting millions of computers via telephone lines, fiber-optic cables, satellites, and microwave links.

It provides access to high-quality information across science, medicine, arts, and more. Users can find data, stay updated on events and achievements, relax, or engage in distance learning — including foreign languages and university programs.

Email remains the most popular service for sending and receiving messages worldwide, containing text, images, audio, and animation, stored on the ISP's server until downloaded.

The World Wide Web, invented by Tim Berners-Lee in March 1989, is a hyperlinked system of millions of pages with text, images, and sound. Users navigate via browsers to explore news, shopping, virtual museums, and more. Anyone with access can consume and contribute content.

Additional tools include FTP (for copying files) and Telnet (for remote access). Audio/video chatting and videoconferencing enable real-time communication.

However, challenges remain. Security is a major concern: data can be intercepted or altered. Despite encryption, most information is transmitted without encoding. Internet addiction — a compulsive need to stay online — is another serious issue. Lack of content control exposes children to inappropriate material.

In conclusion, the Internet offers advantages and disadvantages. We must make informed choices and use its opportunities effectively.
